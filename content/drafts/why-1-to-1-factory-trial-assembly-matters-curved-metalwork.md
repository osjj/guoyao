A 1:1 factory trial assembly puts adjoining parts together at their intended size and orientation before they ship. Its value depends on the question it answers. A trial that shows how two panels meet under raking light serves a different purpose from one that checks whether a handrail and its end return follow a stair frame. Define those questions in the tender so each supplier prices the same checks.

## Decide how much of the assembly to trial

A full-size sample can cover a representative corner, transition or trade interface. A complete trial is more useful when alignment depends on a continuous run, such as a curved balustrade built from several sections or a set of tall panels sharing a recessed seam. Tell the fabricator where a mismatch would be costly to correct on site. Then ask them to propose which parts, adjoining surfaces and support conditions the trial needs.

![Four curved handrail runs on a full-size workshop stair mock-up](img:1)

When a rail follows both the stair plan and its rise, a flat outline captures only part of its shape. Trial-fit the handrail, terminal return and intermediate supports against the agreed stair geometry. Include the relationship to the nosing and landing where those interfaces affect the fit. Ask for a side view as well as a plan view so the handrail rise can be reviewed along the curve.

## Choose datums the workshop and site can both use

Mark the reference points in the fabrication drawings. For a stair, identify the landing levels and centreline, then trace where the geometry changes at the top and bottom. For a long balustrade, use a reference curve with agreed checking points across section breaks. For a curved column, carry a common vertical datum through the frame, panel locations and exposed seams. Give every trial component the same identifying mark it will have on the fitting drawings and packing list.

![Curved balustrade sections checked on a floor jig before fitting](img:2)

A continuous floor layout helps reveal a change in radius or a joint that breaks the finished line. Compare several adjoining sections against the same reference points before evaluating individual brackets. If the frame permits adjustment, record where the allowance is available and what must be fixed before trial assembly. This makes the setup reproducible when sections are erected after delivery.

## Check appearance in the installed orientation

Surface direction changes the way light reads across a double curve. Bring finished neighbours together at the trial and stand the parts in the orientation they will have at the project. Compare a formed reference sample with the adjacent sheets. Review the seam and grain from normal viewing positions, then use glancing light to show local waviness or a change in reflection.

## Record what must be signed off before packing

Use the trial to produce a short acceptance record tied to the project drawings. Make it possible for a reviewer to see the checking points and for the installation team to reconstruct the assembly.

- **Drawing basis.** List the drawing revision, surveyed dimensions used, workshop datum and any site dimensions still to be confirmed.
- **Geometry.** Record the agreed checking points through the transition and the condition of each representative joint, closure and support.
- **Finish.** Identify the approved formed sample, mark the grain direction and photograph adjoining surfaces under the agreed lighting.
- **Access and sequence.** Demonstrate attachment access and the route for adding or removing the final panel. Record the sequence and any special handling or protection.
- **Open items.** Define the remedy and reviewer for each change, what must be rechecked and who releases the assembly for packing.

Include the acceptance criteria, sample responsibilities and trial extent in the enquiry. A workshop record can settle questions about parts and fit under the agreed setup. The project team still needs to verify the surveyed building interfaces and confirm the site installation requirements.
